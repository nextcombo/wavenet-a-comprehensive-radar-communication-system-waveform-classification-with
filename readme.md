# WaveNet: A comprehensive radar communication system waveform classification with improved accuracy and reduced complexity

Introduction: The electromagnetic spectrum is a continuous range of frequencies, typically divided into many bands with different characteristics. In reality, the electromagnetic spectrum is a finite resource, and the amount of bandwidth available in each band is fixed. At the same time, the number of users (e.g., cognitive radio-supported Internet of Things (IoT) devices) that can tolerate interference is limited [1]. In recent years, as more and more devices and systems need to share the electromagnetic spectrum to achieve diverse applications and services, the electromagnetic spectrum has become increasingly congested [2]. This congestion poses significant challenges to the design and deployment of radar and communication systems. Radar systems use electromagnetic waves to detect and track objects, which are widely used in various applications, including weather forecasting, air traffic control, and military surveillance [3]. On the other hand, communication systems use electromagnetic waves to transmit information between connected devices in wireless propagation channels. These two systems usually operate in the same frequency bands, leading to severe interference and degraded performance for both. For example, radar interference can cause communication systems to lose connections or data loss, while communication interference can cause radar systems to miss targets or generate incorrect alerts. To address the challenge of spectrum congestion, new technologies capable of more effectively utilizing spectrum should be developed. For instance, new radar systems using ultra-wideband signals can transmit more information within the same bandwidth. An alternative approach involves the advancement of novel methods for spectrum management, such as implementing spectrum sharing technology that promotes the simultaneous utilization of shared spectrum bands by multiple users, while mitigating potential interference issues. Additionally, the development of next-generation wireless communication also puts pressure on the electromagnetic spectrum. As higher data rates and lower latency are expected in the sixth generation (6G) compared to the fifth generation (5G), more bandwidth resources will be needed [5], [6], [7]. New spectrum sharing technologies are needed to meet the demands of 6G. Recognizing the complexity associated with spectrum congestion and the lack of direct solutions highlights the need for innovative technologies and strategic management approaches to address the issue of spectrum scarcity. By adopting these advancements, the goal is to effectively overcome the challenges posed by spectrum limitations and ensure that radar and communication systems can effectively utilize the electromagnetic spectrum.
The Integrated Radar Communication (IRC) system, combined with intelligent spectrum perception, can enhance the spectral utilization and communication reliability in dynamic environments and further address the issue of spectrum congestion in 6G wireless networks. Cognitive radio networks utilize the IRC system to simultaneously perceive and transmit data. Intelligent spectrum perception enables these radios to dynamically identify available frequency bands, optimize spectrum usage, and avoid interference with existing users. In urban environments, an IRC system equipped with intelligent spectrum sensing is adept at finding idle frequency bands among various radar and communication signals. This capability ensures effective spectral utilization for reliable communication. In autonomous vehicles, the IRC system plays a crucial role. They facilitate communication between vehicles and infrastructure while achieving obstacle detection and navigation through environmental perception via radar functions. Intelligent spectrum sensing ensures reliable communication between potential interferences and optimizes the use of spectrum for both communication and radar tasks. In summary, the IRC system with intelligent spectrum sensing optimizes the spectral usage for communication and sensing tasks within cognitive radio networks, providing efficient spectrum utilization and reliable communication.
The entire IRC system shown in Figure 1 provides a pathway for seamless spectrum sharing between radar and communication systems [8]. Generally, the system consists of radar sensors, communication transceivers, and signal classifiers, with the signal classifier playing a crucial role in distinguishing radar and communication signals based on waveform types [9]. The signal classifier can be implemented using machine learning (ML) algorithms. Once trained successfully on training data, the classifier classifies new signals in real-time at the receiving end. An IRC system with waveform-based signal classification has several advantages. Firstly, it helps improve the performance of radar and communication systems by allocating resources to appropriate signals to minimize interference and ensure effective operation of both systems. Secondly, the system reduces the risk of interference between radar and communication systems, and by accurately classifying signals, it identifies and mitigates potential sources of interference. Thirdly, the system can enhance overall spectrum efficiency by optimizing the use of available resources that share spectrum. In fact, an IRC system with waveform-based signal classification is a promising technology for addressing the severe problem of spectrum congestion, thereby reducing interference and improving the overall system's spectrum efficiency [11].
The waveform classifier is designed based on signal processing and ML techniques to learn complex patterns of radio and communication signals characterized by modulation types. These methods are limited by the fine-grained learning capabilities of traditional ML and the complex knowledge of experts in the field, aiming to optimize the efficiency of feature engineering [12]. To overcome the limitations of ML-based approaches, many recent methods have utilized deep learning (DL) for radar waveform recognition or communication modulation classification separately, without considering the coexistence of shared spectrum and radar communications. Currently, there are few DL-based methods developed for identifying the waveform types of pulsed radars and modulated communication signals within integrated systems. By automatically extracting meaningful features from multi-resolution signal representations, deep neural networks significantly improve system performance, thereby enhancing spectral management and utilization efficiency. For example, Huynh-The et al. [13] proposed a method based on convolutional neural networks (CNN) to automatically identify the waveform types of radar and communication signals using the Wigner-Ville distribution (WVD), which can analyze signals simultaneously in both time and frequency domains. Similarly, work [14] utilized smooth pseudo-WVD (SPWVD) to mitigate crosstalk interference, as well as an innovative CNN to effectively learn underlying spectral features. By combining hierarchical skip connections and two-stage attention, their network effectively utilizes feature diversity, retains gradients, and refines features, thus achieving superior classification accuracy compared to other state-of-the-art methods. Clerico et al. [15] employed long short-term memory (LSTM) networks for classification of communication and radar signals. Although their method leveraged standard public datasets for radar signals, it required training two separate LSTM classifiers for radar and communication signals, meaning that two distinct classification tasks shared a deep architecture.
1) We utilize SPWVD to achieve high-resolution time-frequency signal representation, while mitigating the interference issues present in conventional WVD.
2) To base on the time-frequency representation (i.e., image), we designed a modern deep CNN model, namely Concurrent Radar Communication Waveform Identification Network (WaveNet). The model is designed in three network components through different connection topologies and structures: a) cost-effective feature-aware (CEFA); b) kernel line residual grouping; and c) dual asymmetric channel attention (DACA). The coordination among these modules effectively reduces complexity without affecting accuracy.
3) To conduct performance evaluation, we generated a synthetic signal dataset that includes radar and communication signals of eight waveform types. Through extensive simulations, WaveNet demonstrated effectiveness and robustness in waveform classification under various channel impairments. Moreover, it outperformed other deep models in terms of complexity reduction, with a network size reduced by five times, while achieving an accuracy rate of 92.02%.

WaveNet: Learning Spectral Patterns for Radar Communications Signal Classification
This section introduces the detailed architecture of the proposed deep convolutional network, WaveNet, as shown in Figure 3, which is designed to effectively learn various complex waveform patterns of radio signals during the training phase and automatically identify the waveform styles of received signals in the prediction stage of the IRC system. RadComNet has been designed with a sophisticated architecture involving several advanced structural layers and topological structures to robustly implement waveform-based signal classification of radar and communication signals under multiple channel impairments while consuming less computational resources and being suitable for resource-constrained computing Internet of Things platforms. At the beginning of WaveNet, the input layer size is 224 × 224, identical to the size of the color spectral image generated by SPWVD. Following the input layer is a simple connection structure comprising a convolution (conv) layer with a 3 × 3 filter, a batch normalization (bn) layer, and an activation function relu layer. Notably, the stride (also known as the vertical and horizontal traversing step length) of the conv layer is set to 2, reducing the output feature map size to 32×32. Therefore, the network can analyze underlying features at multiple representative scales and save more computation for subsequent layers.
The core of WaveNet is specified by a series of multiple connected front-end modules, namely CEFA, aimed at improving feature learning efficiency, particularly discovering high discriminative features, and reducing the overall complexity of deep networks for resource-limited devices or mobile computing platforms. CEFA utilizes two key building blocks: 1) kernel residue group (GeR) and 2) DACA, whose architecture mainly consists of two GeR blocks and one DACA block sequentially connected. These modules play different roles in achieving accurate waveform classification.
1) GeR: The WaveNet architecture effectively processes feature learning through the use of grouped convolutional blocks, significantly reducing both trainable parameters and memory usage compared to standard convolutions. Notably, this is crucial for resource-constrained devices. Moreover, the GeR blocks include skip connections that effectively address the vanishing gradient problem that could hinder deep network training. By allowing gradients to flow directly from later layers to earlier ones, these connections ensure that all layers are effectively contributing to feature extraction.
2) DACA: Following the GeR blocks, the DACA block further enhances the extraction of features for waveform classification. DACA analyzes the time and frequency axes of the waveform spectrum representation. This allows it to identify the most discriminative time-related characteristics and spectral components to differentiate waveform categories. Additionally, DACA utilizes asymmetric pooling operations to capture different representations in both the temporal and frequency domains. Finally, the sigmoid activation function emphasizes information channels in the features, suppresses less relevant information, and ensures the network focuses on the most critical aspects of classification.

The Deep ConvNet architecture used for waveform classification in radar communication systems consists of three main CEFA modules, constructed through cascading connections within the network body. As detailed in the architecture, each CEFA has two GeR blocks and one DACA block, with their detailed structures drawn alongside. The GeR is constructed using regular convolutions with varying filter sizes and residual connections. Then, the features extracted by the GeR blocks are emphasized using the multi-level attention mechanism in DACA.
GeR: In the detailed architecture shown in Figure 3, GeR consists of two sets of sequentially connected layers. Each set includes a unit convolutional layer (also known as a convolutional layer with 1 × 1 filters) and a grouped convolutional layer for general feature extraction, followed by a batch normalization layer and a ReLU layer. In addition to reducing the number of generated feature maps and computational complexity and memory requirements, the unit convolutional layer also activates checkerboard techniques to learn more effective representations of the input image. For the grouped convolutional layer, the input channels are divided into groups to apply sliding convolutional filters. For each group, the grouped convolutional layer convolutes the input along the vertical and horizontal directions of the traversing filter. The output is calculated as the dot product of the filter weights and the input points plus the sum of the bias term. This layer independently collects the convolutions of each group. The number of output channels for the grouped convolutional layer is defined by k × g, where k is the number of filters per group and g is the number of groups. The number of channel groups is automatically set equal to the number of input channels divided by the number of groups. It is worth noting that the total number of trainable parameters in the grouped conv layer is g times less than the total number of trainable parameters in the regular conv layer, thus significantly reducing network size (as measured by the total number of trainable parameters).
The vanishing gradient problem is a common and serious issue in deep learning, where the gradient of the loss function about the weight of an early layer becomes very small. This phenomenon reduces the efficiency of parameter learning in the early layers (i.e., almost no weight update activity during backpropagation). Bypass connections help alleviate the vanishing gradient problem by providing a direct path for the gradient from later layers to earlier layers.

A. Data generation and simulation settings
To evaluate the classification performance of WaveNet, we generated custom datasets for eight waveform types using MATLAB (see Section III-A), specifically designed for waveform features of interest in this study.
Dataset contains 262,144 signals across 32,768 waveform types. The dataset is randomly divided into three categories: 70% for training, 15% for validation, and 15% for testing. As shown in Table II, with a sampling frequency of fs = 100 MHz, we generate each signal with random unique features and enhance them with various challenging channel impairments to make them more realistic. For example, we randomly adjust the number of samples, bandwidth, code length, and the number of phase codes per signal in the radar waveform configuration. Additionally, we use multipath fading channels to represent real phenomena in wireless communication, such as multipath scattering effects with path delay [0, 1.8, 3.4]/fs corresponding to average path gain [0, -2, -10] dB. We also add AWGN with SNR ranging from [-10:25] dB, with a step size of 5 dB (4096 signals generated for each SNR level per waveform type), maximum Doppler shift of 70 Hz, and maximum clock offset of 5 ppm (parts per million). With a sampling rate of 200 kHz and center frequency of 900 MHz, each communication signal has 1024 samples. We trained and evaluated WaveNet on a personal computing platform equipped with a 3.80 GHz CPU, 64 GB RAM, and a single NVIDIA GTX 3060 Ti GPU. We ran multiple independent tests for model training, validation, and performance measurement to calculate the average accuracy on the test set.
We present three simulation results and discuss them in detail.
1) The first simulation tested the classification accuracy of WaveNet on the test set under a comprehensive damaged transmission channel.
2) The second simulation studied the accuracy of WaveNet under different Doppler spread conditions, varying the number of CEFA modules, and conducting ablations of group convolution and channel attention.
3) Simulation results show that compared to existing dynamic link-based waveform classification methods, the proposed method achieves higher classification accuracy, lower classification complexity, and better classification precision, demonstrating its effectiveness as a new waveform classification method.
As a key metric for evaluating waveform classification performance, the average accuracy (also known as the corrected classification percentage) is calculated on test sets with multiple training, validation, and prediction run times.

Conclusion In this paper, we propose an effective waveform classification method for IRC 6G systems to alleviate spectrum congestion. We learn an advanced deep network using SPWVD for TFA. Notably, we introduce WaveNet, an economically efficient deep model with a specialized CNN architecture that integrates two well-designed modules, namely Geer connection and DACA, into a high-performance feature learning module, significantly reducing complexity without sacrificing accuracy. Simulation results show that WaveNet's effectiveness and robustness, achieving an overall classification accuracy of 92.02% on datasets with damaged signals of eight different radar and communication waveform types. Compared to other deep models, WaveNet maintains the lowest architectural complexity by 5x smaller network size while performing slightly better than them by about 0.5%, making our proposed network more suitable for resource-constrained IoT devices.
Although this work demonstrates the effectiveness of WaveNet in classifying radar and communication signals using SPWVD spectral graphs, its core functionality extends beyond this specific field. WaveNet's ability to process complex signals makes it a valuable tool for various waveform classification tasks: 1) electronic warfare, where WaveNet's capability to classify complex signals could be useful for identifying and categorizing various communication and radar signals used in electronic warfare scenarios, and 2) acoustic scene classification, where WaveNet could potentially be applied to tasks like categorizing different environmental sounds (e.g., traffic noise, speech, and music). In the future, we aim to optimize WaveNet's architecture through cutting-edge techniques such as channel pruning and knowledge distillation, thereby reducing model size while maintaining accuracy. Additionally, we plan to explore the broader application of WaveNet in signal classification beyond radar and communication, thus expanding its practicality.
